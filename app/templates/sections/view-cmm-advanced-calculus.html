Vectors, Complex Numbers & Differential Equations

Lecture 1. The geometric and algebraic toolkit behind quant finance: vectors and their products, the complex plane and De Moivre / Euler machinery, and the first- and second-order ODE solution methods that pricing and rate models rely on.

1  Vectors

1.1 – 1.4  Vectors, Length & Unit Vectors

Vectors have magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, acceleration, force); scalars have magnitude only (distance, speed, density, temperature). A vector in $n$ dimensions is

$$\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n,$$

with components $v_i$. Two vectors are equal iff they have the same dimension and equal corresponding components.

Arithmetic. For $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ and scalar $k$:

$$\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}=(u_1+v_1,\dots,u_n+v_n), \qquad k\mathbf{u}=(ku_1,\dots,ku_n).$$

Length / norm. By Pythagoras, the magnitude is

$$\|\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt{v_1^2 + v_2^2 + \cdots + v_n^2},$$

and any non-zero vector can be written $\mathbf{v} = \|\mathbf{v}\|\,\hat{\mathbf{v}}$ where $\hat{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{v}/\|\mathbf{v}\|$ is the unit vector ($\|\hat{\mathbf{v}}\|=1$). The axes $\mathbf{i}=(1,0,0)$, $\mathbf{j}=(0,1,0)$, $\mathbf{k}=(0,0,1)$ are the standard unit vectors, so a point $P(x,y,z)$ has position vector $\mathbf{r}=x\mathbf{i}+y\mathbf{j}+z\mathbf{k}$.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: A vector is just an ordered list of numbers with a geometric meaning. In finance a portfolio of $n$ holdings, or a vector of $n$ asset returns, lives in $\mathbb{R}^n$ exactly like a physical displacement.

  • The norm generalizes "length" to any dimension — it is the same formula used for the size of a return vector or a risk-factor shock.
  • Unit vectors strip out size and keep only direction — useful whenever you care about a mix (weights) rather than a notional.

Scalar (Dot) Product

The dot product combines two vectors into a scalar and encodes the angle between them:

$$\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} u_i v_i = \|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta.$$

Rearranging gives the angle $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\,\|\mathbf{v}\|}$. When $\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ the vectors are orthogonal and $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=0$.

1.5  Vector (Cross) Product $\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}$

Unlike the dot product, the cross product returns another vector, computed via the determinant mnemonic

$$\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}.$$

Geometrically $\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b} = \|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf{u}}$, orthogonal to both $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$, so $(\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{a}=(\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathbf{b}=0$ and $\mathbf{b}\wedge\mathbf{a}=-\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}$. For the basis vectors $\mathbf{i}\wedge\mathbf{j}=\mathbf{k}$, $\mathbf{j}\wedge\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{i}$, $\mathbf{k}\wedge\mathbf{i}=\mathbf{j}$.

1.5.2  Triple Products, Determinants & Area

The scalar triple product is the determinant

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}\wedge\mathbf{c}) = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix},$$

whose absolute value is the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the three vectors. Useful determinant rules: swapping two rows changes the sign; two equal rows give $0$; a common factor in a row/column factors out; adding a multiple of one row to another leaves the determinant unchanged (the basis of row reduction).

Because $\|\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}\| = \|\mathbf{a}\|\,\|\mathbf{b}\|\sin\theta = 2\,(\text{area of }\triangle OAB)$, the area of a triangle with edge vectors $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}$ is

$$\text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\|\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}\|.$$

2  Complex Numbers

2.1 – 2.5  Background, Arithmetic & Polar Form

Not every polynomial has a real root: $x^2+1=0$ forces us to define $i=\sqrt{-1}$. The quadratic $ax^2+bx+c=0$ then always solves over $\mathbb{C}$. For $z_1=a+ib,\ z_2=c+id$:

$$z_1\pm z_2 = (a\pm c)+i(b\pm d), \qquad z_1 z_2 = (ac-bd)+i(ad+bc),$$

and a handy result is $\dfrac{1}{i} = -i$. With $z=x+iy$, the modulus and argument are

$$|z| = r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}, \qquad \theta = \arg z = \arctan\tfrac{y}{x},$$

(principal range $-\pi<\theta\le\pi$). The polar form is $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$, and the conjugate $\bar z = x-iy$ gives $\operatorname{Re}z=\tfrac{z+\bar z}{2}$, $\operatorname{Im}z=\tfrac{z-\bar z}{2i}$, $|z|^2 = z\bar z$.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: A complex number is a point in a 2-D plane that you can also multiply. The modulus is its distance from the origin (Pythagoras again); the argument is its angle.

  • Why quants care: Fourier and characteristic-function option pricing (Heston, Carr–Madan) live entirely in the complex plane.
  • Polar form turns multiplication into "multiply the lengths, add the angles" — the key simplification behind everything below.

2.6  Euler's Formula

For any angle $\theta$, $\;\exp(i\theta) = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.$ Substituting $x=i\theta$ into the Taylor series $e^x = \sum x^n/n!$ and separating real and imaginary parts recovers the series for $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$:

$$e^{i\theta} = \underbrace{\left(1 - \tfrac{\theta^2}{2!} + \tfrac{\theta^4}{4!} - \cdots\right)}_{\cos\theta} + i\underbrace{\left(\theta - \tfrac{\theta^3}{3!} + \tfrac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\right)}_{\sin\theta}.$$

Hence in mod–arg form $z = re^{i\theta}$, and for $z_1=r_1e^{i\theta_1},\ z_2=r_2e^{i\theta_2}$:

$$z_1 z_2 = r_1 r_2\, e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}, \qquad \frac{z_1}{z_2} = \frac{r_1}{r_2}\, e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)} \quad (z_2\neq 0),$$

so $|z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2|$ and $\arg(z_1 z_2) = \arg z_1 + \arg z_2$. Periodicity gives $\exp(i\theta) = \exp\!\big(i(\theta+2k\pi)\big)$ for $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ — the source of multiple roots below.

2.7  Circular & Hyperbolic Functions

For any $z\in\mathbb{C}$, Euler's formula defines the generalised circular and hyperbolic functions

$$\sin z = \frac{e^{iz}-e^{-iz}}{2i}, \quad \cos z = \frac{e^{iz}+e^{-iz}}{2}, \quad \sinh z = \frac{e^{z}-e^{-z}}{2}, \quad \cosh z = \frac{e^{z}+e^{-z}}{2}.$$

These give the bridges between the two families:

$$\sinh(iz)=i\sin z, \quad \cosh(iz)=\cos z, \quad \sin(iz)=i\sinh z, \quad \cos(iz)=\cosh z.$$

2.8  De Moivre's Theorem

For integer $n$,

$$(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta,$$

proved by induction on $n$ (and extended to negatives and rationals). Two standard uses: expanding $\cos n\theta,\ \sin n\theta$ as powers of $\cos\theta,\sin\theta$, and — via $z+\tfrac{1}{z}=2\cos\theta$, $z^n+\tfrac{1}{z^n}=2\cos n\theta$ — the reverse, writing powers like $\cos^4\theta$ as sums of $\cos n\theta$ for easy integration.

2.8.2  Roots of Complex Numbers

An $n$th root of $z = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ satisfies $w^n=z$, i.e. $w=z^{1/n}$. Using periodicity and De Moivre,

$$z^{1/n} = r^{1/n}\left[\cos\!\left(\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n}\right) + i\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta+2k\pi}{n}\right)\right], \qquad k=0,1,\dots,n-1,$$

giving exactly $n$ roots equally spaced by $2\pi/n$ on a circle of radius $r^{1/n}$. The $n$th roots of unity ($z=1$) are $\exp(2k\pi i/n)$. Example: the cube roots of $1+i=\sqrt{2}\,e^{i\pi/4}$ are $(1+i)^{1/3} = 2^{1/6}\exp\!\big(i(8k+1)\pi/12\big)$, $k=0,1,2$.

Triangle Inequality

For any $z_1,z_2\in\mathbb{C}$, $\;|z_1+z_2|\le|z_1|+|z_2|.$ The proof rests on the lemma $\operatorname{Re}z\le|z|$ (since $|x|\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$), then

$$|z_1+z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(z_1\bar z_2) \le |z_1|^2+|z_2|^2+2|z_1||z_2| = (|z_1|+|z_2|)^2.$$

3  First-Order Differential Equations

3.1  Homogeneous Equations

$f(x,y)$ is homogeneous of degree $k$ if $f(tx,ty)=t^k f(x,y)$. The ODE $\tfrac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y)$ is homogeneous when $f$ is homogeneous of degree $0$. Substituting $y=vx$, so $\tfrac{dy}{dx}=v + x\tfrac{dv}{dx}$, reduces it to the separable equation

$$x\frac{dv}{dx} = f(1,v) - v \;\Longrightarrow\; \int\frac{dv}{f(1,v)-v} = \int\frac{dx}{x}.$$

Reducible form. $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{ax+by+c}{Ax+By+C}$ is made homogeneous by shifting $x=X+h,\ y=Y+k$ where $(h,k)$ is the intersection of the two lines (provided they are not parallel), then setting $Y=VX$.

3.2  Bernoulli Equation

The non-linear ODE

$$y' + P(x)\,y = Q(x)\,y^n$$

is linear for $n=0,1$. For $n\ge 2$, divide by $y^n$ and substitute $z = y^{1-n}$, so $z' = (1-n)y^{-n}y'$, which linearizes it to

$$z' + (1-n)P(x)\,z = (1-n)Q(x),$$

solvable with the integrating factor $R(x)=\exp\!\int (1-n)P\,dx$.

3.3  Exact Equations

$M(x,y)\,dx + N(x,y)\,dy = 0$ is exact if there exists $G(x,y)$ with $dG = M\,dx + N\,dy$; the solution is then simply $G(x,y)=\text{const}$. The test for exactness is

$$\frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}.$$

One recovers $G$ by integrating $M$ in $x$ (holding $y$ fixed) and $N$ in $y$ (holding $x$ fixed), then matching the two expressions.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: Each method is a trick to turn a hard ODE into one you can integrate directly — substitution (homogeneous, Bernoulli) or recognising a total derivative (exact).

  • Integrating factor: when $\partial_y M \neq \partial_x N$, multiply through by a cleverly chosen $\mu$ to force exactness — the same idea used to solve linear first-order ODEs.
  • Finance link: short-rate and bond-price models are first-order ODEs/PDEs; these are the hand tools before the numerical ones.

3.3.1  Reducible to Exact — Integrating Factors

If $M\,dx+N\,dy=0$ is not exact, an integrating factor restores exactness. When $\dfrac{M_y - N_x}{N}$ is a function of $x$ alone, $\mu(x)=\exp\!\int\dfrac{M_y-N_x}{N}\,dx$; when $\dfrac{N_x - M_y}{M}$ is a function of $y$ alone, $\mu(y)=\exp\!\int\dfrac{N_x-M_y}{M}\,dy$. Multiplying through by $\mu$ yields an exact equation solved as above.

4  Second-Order Linear ODEs — The D-Operator Method

4.1  Complementary Function + Particular Integral

For the constant-coefficient problem

$$a\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + b\frac{dy}{dx} + cy = g(x),$$

the general solution is $y = y_c + y_p$: the complementary function $y_c$ (from the auxiliary equation $a\lambda^2+b\lambda+c=0$) plus a particular integral $y_p$. Writing $D\equiv\tfrac{d}{dx}$ and $L\equiv aD^2+bD+c$, the equation is $Ly=g(x)$ and

$$y_p = \frac{1}{aD^2+bD+c}\,g(x),$$

treating the inverse operator algebraically. Key techniques:

  • Constant / polynomial RHS: for $g=K$ use $\tfrac{1}{L(0)}K$; for polynomials expand $\tfrac{1}{L(D)}$ by the binomial theorem.
  • Exponential RHS — shift theorem: $f(D)\big(e^{ax}v\big) = e^{ax}f(D+a)\,v$, so $\tfrac{1}{L(D)}e^{ax} = \tfrac{1}{L(a)}e^{ax}$ when $L(a)\neq 0$. E.g. $\tfrac{1}{D^2+2D+4}e^{3x} = \tfrac{1}{19}e^{3x}$.
  • Trigonometric RHS: use Euler's identity, $\tfrac{1}{F(D)}v\cos px = \operatorname{Re}\,e^{ipx}\tfrac{1}{F(D+ip)}v$, and likewise $\sin px$ with $\operatorname{Im}$.

Q9  Solving Complex Equations

For $z=x+iy$, split into real/imaginary parts using $\cos z = \cos x\cosh y - i\sin x\sinh y$ etc.

(i) $\cos z = 4$: equating parts gives $\sin x\sinh y=0$ and $\cos x\cosh y=4$. Since $\cosh y\ge 1$ we need $\cos x = \pm1$, i.e. $x=n\pi$, and then $\cosh y = 4$ so $y = \pm\operatorname{arccosh}4$ — a real cosine of $4$ is impossible, but a complex one is not. The same real/imaginary splitting solves $\sin z = 2$, $\cosh 2z = 2i$ and $\cot z = 1$.

Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Vectors carry magnitude and direction; the dot product gives angles/projections ($\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\cos\theta$), the cross product gives an orthogonal vector and areas ($\tfrac12\|\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{b}\|$).
  • The scalar triple product is a determinant measuring volume; row operations are its computational engine.
  • Complex numbers complete the algebra of polynomials; polar / Euler form $z=re^{i\theta}$ turns multiplication into add-the-angles and powers/roots into De Moivre.
  • $n$th roots are $n$ equally spaced points on a circle; circular and hyperbolic functions are two faces of the same exponential.
  • First-order ODEs are tamed by substitution (homogeneous, Bernoulli) or by recognising/forcing an exact form (integrating factors).
  • Constant-coefficient second-order ODEs split as $y=y_c+y_p$; the D-operator with the shift theorem and Euler's identity delivers the particular integral mechanically.
