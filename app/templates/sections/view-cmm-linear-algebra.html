Mathematical Methods • Linear Algebra

Vector Spaces, Matrices & Eigenvalues

From linear systems and abstract vector spaces, through the full machinery of matrices, determinants and inverses, to the eigenvalue problem and diagonalization — the structures behind covariance, PCA, factor models and the numerical core of every quant library.

1  Linear Systems & Vector Spaces

1.1  Review of Linear Equations

A system of $m$ equations in $n$ unknowns is written compactly as $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$, where $A$ is the coefficient matrix. We solve by forming the augmented matrix $[A\,|\,\mathbf{b}]$ and applying Gaussian elimination (elementary row operations) to reach row-echelon form:

$$\left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 2 & 1 & -1 & 8 \\ -3 & -1 & 2 & -11 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 & -3 \end{array}\right] \longrightarrow \left[\begin{array}{ccc|c} 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -1 \end{array}\right].$$

The solution is unique when the coefficient matrix reduces to the identity (full rank); otherwise the system has no solution or infinitely many.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: Almost every quantitative task — calibrating a curve, fitting a regression, solving a PDE on a grid — ends up as "solve $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$." Row reduction is the universal hand-method behind the numerical solvers.

  • Unique vs. not: whether a solution exists and is unique is a rank question — the same idea that flags a singular (un-invertible) covariance matrix.
  • Bond bootstrapping and least-squares curve fits are linear systems at heart.

1.2  Vector Spaces

A vector space $V$ over a field (here $\mathbb{R}$) is a set with two operations — vector addition and scalar multiplication — satisfying eight axioms. Addition makes $V$ an abelian group:

  • $u+v = v+u$ (commutative)
  • $(u+v)+w = u+(v+w)$ (associative)
  • $\exists\,\mathbf{0}$ with $v+\mathbf{0}=v$
  • $\exists\,(-v)$ with $v+(-v)=\mathbf{0}$
  • $k(u+v) = ku+kv$
  • $(k+l)v = kv+lv$
  • $(kl)v = k(lv)$
  • $1\cdot v = v$

Examples: $\mathbb{R}^n$; the polynomials $P_n$ of degree $\le n$; the set of all $m\times n$ matrices; real-valued functions on an interval. All obey the same axioms, which is what lets one theory serve them all.

2.1  Subspaces

$U\subseteq V$ is a subspace if it is itself a vector space under the inherited operations. The practical test: $U$ is non-empty and closed under addition and scalar multiplication,

$$u_1,u_2\in U \Rightarrow u_1+u_2\in U, \qquad k\in\mathbb{R},\,u\in U \Rightarrow ku\in U.$$

Equivalently, $u_1,u_2\in U \Rightarrow \alpha u_1+\beta u_2\in U$ for all scalars. Examples: upper-triangular matrices; symmetric matrices; functions with $f(-1)=f(1)$; the solution set of a homogeneous linear ODE.

2.1.1  Linear Dependence & Independence

A linear combination of $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is $a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n$. The set is linearly independent if

$$a_1v_1 + a_2v_2 + \cdots + a_nv_n = \mathbf{0} \;\Longrightarrow\; a_1=a_2=\cdots=a_n=0,$$

i.e. the only way to make zero is the trivial way. If some non-trivial combination vanishes, the set is dependent — one vector is redundant (expressible via the others).

3.2  Span, Basis & Dimension

The span (generated subspace) of $\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$ is the set of all their linear combinations, $U=\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}=\{a_1v_1+\cdots+a_nv_n\}$ — always a subspace. A basis is a spanning set that is also linearly independent:

  • every $v\in V$ is a unique combination of the basis vectors;
  • removing any basis vector breaks spanning; adding any vector breaks independence.

The number of basis vectors is the dimension $\dim V$ — an invariant: every basis of $V$ has the same size. (E.g. $\dim\mathbb{R}^n=n$, $\dim P_n=n+1$.)

3  Application: Solution Spaces of Linear ODEs

A linear ODE of order $\ge 2$, $\;\mathcal{D}y = 0$ with linear differential operator $\mathcal{D}$, has a solution set that is a vector space: any linear combination of solutions is again a solution. For a constant-coefficient $n$th-order equation, the auxiliary equation supplies $n$ independent solutions (real / repeated / complex-root cases), which form a basis for the $n$-dimensional solution space.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: "Basis" and "dimension" make precise the idea of independent drivers. A yield curve moving in 100 maturities is really driven by ~3 independent factors — a low-dimensional basis (level, slope, curvature).

  • Independence = no redundancy: a dependent set of risk factors means one is a combination of the others (multicollinearity).
  • Solution spaces: the same abstraction means "all solutions of this model" is itself a space you can pick a convenient basis for.

2  Matrices, Determinants & Inverses

1  Sum & Intersection of Subspaces

For subspaces $S,T\subseteq V$, both $S\cap T$ (vectors in both) and $S+T=\{s+t: s\in S,\,t\in T\}$ are subspaces. Their dimensions obey the dimension formula

$$\dim(S+T) = \dim S + \dim T - \dim(S\cap T).$$

When $S\cap T=\{\mathbf{0}\}$ the sum is direct, $S\oplus T$, and dimensions simply add.

2  Matrices as Linear Transformations

An $m\times n$ matrix is a rectangular array $A=(a_{ij})$. Its deeper meaning: a linear transformation $T:V\to W$ obeying $T(\alpha u+\beta v)=\alpha T(u)+\beta T(v)$ is represented, once bases are fixed, by a matrix acting as $\mathbf{x}\mapsto A\mathbf{x}$. Composition of transformations corresponds to matrix multiplication.

Algebra. Addition and scalar multiplication act entry-wise; the product $AB$ has entries

$$(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k} a_{ik}b_{kj}$$

(row $i$ of $A$ dotted with column $j$ of $B$) — defined only when the inner dimensions match, and not commutative in general.

Special Matrices

  • Identity $I$: $a_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$ (the Kronecker delta), $AI=IA=A$.
  • Triangular: zeros below (upper) or above (lower) the diagonal.
  • Diagonal: non-zero only on the diagonal.
  • Symmetric: $A^{T}=A$;  skew-symmetric: $A^{T}=-A$.
  • Orthogonal: $A^{T}A=I$, so $A^{-1}=A^{T}$.

Any square matrix splits into symmetric + skew parts: $A=\tfrac12(A+A^{T})+\tfrac12(A-A^{T})$.

Rank, Row Echelon Form & the Solvability of $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$

Elementary row operations — (i) swap two rows, (ii) multiply a row by a non-zero scalar, (iii) add a multiple of one row to another — bring $A$ to row-echelon form (leading 1's stepping right and down) and then reduced row-echelon form. The number of non-zero rows is the rank, equal to both the row-space and column-space dimension.

Determinants, Minors & Cofactors

The determinant of a square matrix is computed by cofactor (Laplace) expansion along any row or column:

$$\det A = \sum_{j} a_{ij}\,C_{ij}, \qquad C_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}M_{ij},$$

where the minor $M_{ij}$ is the determinant of $A$ with row $i$ and column $j$ deleted. Key properties:

  • $\det(A^{T})=\det A$;  $\det(AB)=\det A\,\det B$;
  • a triangular matrix's determinant is the product of its diagonal;
  • swapping two rows flips the sign; equal rows $\Rightarrow \det=0$.

Inverses & the Adjoint

$A^{-1}$ satisfies $AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I$ and exists iff $\det A\neq 0$. It is unique, and $(AB)^{-1}=B^{-1}A^{-1}$. Via the adjoint (transpose of the cofactor matrix),

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\,\operatorname{adj}(A), \qquad \operatorname{adj}(A)=(C_{ij})^{T}.$$

For the $2\times 2$ case this is the familiar

$$\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}.$$

Cramer's Rule

For a square system $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ with $\det A\neq 0$, each unknown is a ratio of determinants:

$$x_i = \frac{\det A_i}{\det A},$$

where $A_i$ is $A$ with its $i$th column replaced by $\mathbf{b}$. Elegant for small systems and theory; row reduction is preferred numerically for large ones.

Transpose & Orthogonal / Rotation Matrices

The transpose obeys $(A^{T})^{T}=A$, $(A+B)^{T}=A^{T}+B^{T}$, and the order-reversing $(AB)^{T}=B^{T}A^{T}$. An orthogonal matrix ($A^{T}A=I$) preserves lengths and angles; the planar rotation matrix is

$$R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.$$

Rotations compose by adding angles, which matrix multiplication reproduces exactly:

$$R(\phi)\,R(\theta) = R(\theta+\phi).$$

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: A matrix is a verb — it does something to vectors (rotate, stretch, project). The determinant measures how it scales volume; a zero determinant means it collapses space and cannot be undone.

  • Symmetric matrices are the covariance matrices of finance — and their special structure (real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors) is what makes PCA work.
  • $\det A=0$ is the warning light: a non-invertible covariance matrix means perfectly redundant assets.

3  Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

The Eigenvalue Problem

A non-zero vector $\mathbf{x}$ is an eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ if

$$A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x} \;\Longleftrightarrow\; (A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}.$$

A non-trivial solution exists only when the matrix is singular, giving the characteristic equation

$$\det(A-\lambda I)=0,$$

a degree-$n$ polynomial in $\lambda$ whose roots are the eigenvalues. For each $\lambda$, the solutions of $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ form the eigenspace. Similar matrices ($B=P^{-1}AP$) share the same characteristic polynomial and eigenvalues.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: An eigenvector is a direction the matrix leaves pointing the same way, only stretched by its eigenvalue. These special directions reveal a matrix's intrinsic structure.

  • PCA: the eigenvectors of a covariance matrix are the principal components; the eigenvalues are the variance each explains.
  • Yield-curve factors: the top eigenvectors are level, slope and curvature — a handful explain almost all movement.

Diagonalization

If an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors, stacking them as the columns of $P$ and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $D$ gives

$$P^{-1}AP = D = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n), \qquad A = PDP^{-1}.$$

This is guaranteed when the eigenvalues are distinct. Diagonalization makes powers trivial — $A^{k}=PD^{k}P^{-1}$ — which is the engine behind matrix exponentials, Markov-chain limits and covariance factorization.

Cayley–Hamilton Theorem

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation: if $p(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)$, then

$$p(A) = \mathbf{0}.$$

1.1  The Minimum Polynomial

The minimal polynomial $m_A(\lambda)$ is the monic polynomial of least degree with $m_A(A)=\mathbf{0}$. It divides the characteristic polynomial and shares its roots (the eigenvalues), but may have lower multiplicities.

Example: for a matrix with characteristic polynomial $(\lambda-3)(\lambda-2)^{2}$ that is nonetheless diagonalizable, the minimal polynomial is $(\lambda-3)(\lambda-2)$ — the repeated factor appears only once.

Worked Example — Eigen-decomposition of a $2\times2$ Matrix

For $A=\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, the characteristic equation is

$$\det(A-\lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-2 = \lambda^{2}-7\lambda+10 = (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2)=0,$$

so $\lambda_1=5,\ \lambda_2=2$. Solving $(A-\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{0}$ gives eigenvectors $\mathbf{x}_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x}_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\end{pmatrix}$. With distinct eigenvalues $A$ is diagonalizable as $A=PDP^{-1}$, $P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-2\end{pmatrix}$, $D=\operatorname{diag}(5,2)$.

Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Linear systems $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ are solved by row reduction; solvability and uniqueness are governed by rank.
  • A vector space abstracts addition + scaling; subspaces are closed subsets, and span / independence / basis / dimension pin down "independent directions" exactly.
  • Matrices are linear transformations; multiplication is composition. Special structure (symmetric, orthogonal, triangular) carries special properties.
  • Determinants (cofactor expansion) detect invertibility; $A^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{\det A}\operatorname{adj}A$, and Cramer's rule solves square systems via determinant ratios.
  • The eigenvalue problem $A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}$ — via $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ — reveals invariant directions; diagonalization $A=PDP^{-1}$ powers PCA, factor models and matrix functions.
  • Cayley–Hamilton and the minimal polynomial express matrix functions as low-degree polynomials in $A$.
