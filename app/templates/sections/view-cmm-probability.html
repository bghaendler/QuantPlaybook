Mathematical Methods • Probability

Probability Theory

From sample spaces and Kolmogorov's axioms, through random variables, expectation and the moment / characteristic-function machinery, to the key distributions and the limit theorems (Chebyshev, LLN, CLT) that underpin risk-neutral pricing, Monte Carlo and risk measurement.

1  Foundations of Probability

1.1  Experiments, Sample Spaces & Events

An experiment is any process with an uncertain outcome. The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes; an event $A\subseteq\Omega$ is a subset of outcomes. Two early definitions of probability:

  • Classical: for equally likely outcomes, $$P(A) = \frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}.$$
  • Frequentist: $P(A)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\dfrac{n_A}{n}$, the long-run relative frequency.

1.2  The Three Axioms (Kolmogorov)

Modern probability is built axiomatically. A probability measure $P$ assigns to events numbers satisfying:

  • Axiom 1: $P(A)\ge 0$ for every event $A$.
  • Axiom 2: $P(\Omega)=1$ (certainty).
  • Axiom 3: for mutually exclusive $A_1,A_2,\dots$, $$P\!\left(\bigcup_i A_i\right) = \sum_i P(A_i).$$

From these follow $P(A^c)=1-P(A)$, $P(\varnothing)=0$, and $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$.

1.3  Counting: Permutations & Combinations

When outcomes are equally likely, computing probabilities becomes counting. Order-sensitive arrangements are permutations; order-blind selections are combinations:

$$^nP_r = \frac{n!}{(n-r)!}, \qquad \binom{n}{r} = {}^nC_r = \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!}.$$

Assignment (Q1)

Four-digit numbers from $0$–$9$: with repetition $9\times10^3=9000$; without repetition $9\times9\times8\times7=4536$; last digit $0$, no repetition $1\times9\times8\times7=504$.

Combinations (Q2)

"Words" of 5 consonants (from 9) and 3 vowels (from 6): $8!\,\binom{9}{5}\binom{6}{3} = 40320\times126\times20$.

Cards (Q3)

From 5 of 52 cards, $P(\text{4 kings}) = \dfrac{\binom{4}{4}\binom{48}{1}}{\binom{52}{5}} = \dfrac{1}{54{,}145}$.

1.4  Conditional Probability, Independence & Bayes

The probability of $A$ given $B$ has occurred is

$$P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B)>0.$$

$A$ and $B$ are independent iff $P(A\cap B)=P(A)\,P(B)$, equivalently $P(A\mid B)=P(A)$. Combining the definition with the law of total probability gives Bayes' theorem:

$$P(A\mid B) = \frac{P(B\mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B)=\sum_i P(B\mid A_i)P(A_i).$$

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: Probability is a consistent way to put numbers on uncertainty. Bayes' theorem is how you update those numbers when new evidence arrives — the backbone of calibration and signal-filtering.

  • Independence is a strong assumption: correlated defaults in a CDO are exactly what happens when it fails.
  • Bayes in practice: updating a regime probability or a default probability as fresh data lands.

Worked Example — The Double-Headed Coin (Bayes, Q5)

Three coins, one double-headed ($T$ = "picked the two-headed coin"). The first toss is a head with

$$P(F_1) = P(T)\cdot 1 + P(T^c)\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac13\cdot1 + \tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac23.$$

After $n$ heads, Bayes gives the posterior $p_n = P(T\mid F_n) = \dfrac{\tfrac13\cdot 1}{\tfrac13\cdot 1 + \tfrac23\left(\tfrac12\right)^n} \to 1$ as $n\to\infty$ — a long run of heads makes the loaded coin near-certain, exactly as intuition expects.

2  Random Variables & Distributions

2.1  Random Variables, PMF, PDF & CDF

A random variable $X$ maps outcomes to numbers, $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$. For a discrete $X$, the probability mass function $p(x)=P(X=x)$ satisfies $\sum_x p(x)=1$. For a continuous $X$, the probability density $f(x)\ge0$ satisfies $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx=1$ and

$$P(aBoth share the cumulative distribution function

$$F(x) = P(X\le x), \qquad f(x)=\frac{dF}{dx}.$$

2.2  Joint, Marginal & Conditional

For two variables, the joint law is $p(x,y)$ or $f(x,y)$. Marginals recover a single variable by summing/integrating out the other:

$$f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x,y)\,dy.$$

The conditional density is $f(x\mid y)=\dfrac{f(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}$, and $X,Y$ are independent iff the joint factorizes:

$$f(x,y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y).$$

3  Expectation, Moments & Generating Functions

3.1  Mean, Variance & Higher Moments

The expectation (mean) is the probability-weighted average

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_x x\,p(x) \quad\text{(discrete)}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,f(x)\,dx \quad\text{(continuous)}.$$

Expectation is linear: $\mathbb{E}[aX+bY]=a\mathbb{E}[X]+b\mathbb{E}[Y]$. The variance and its companions are

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2] = \mathbb{E}[X^2]-\big(\mathbb{E}[X]\big)^2,$$

with skewness and kurtosis the standardized third and fourth moments. For two variables,

$$\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y], \qquad \rho_{XY} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\,\sigma_Y}.$$

3.2  Moment & Characteristic Functions

The moment generating function packages all moments:

$$M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX}\big], \qquad \mathbb{E}[X^n] = M_X^{(n)}(0).$$

In particular $\mathbb{E}[X]=M_X'(0)$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=M_X''(0)-M_X'(0)^2$. The characteristic function $\varphi_X(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]$ always exists (it is the Fourier transform of the density) and uniquely determines the distribution — the engine of transform-based option pricing.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: The mean is the expected return, the variance is risk, skew and kurtosis are the asymmetry and fat tails that simple models miss.

  • Risk-neutral pricing is "price = discounted expectation" — an integral against a probability density.
  • Characteristic functions are why Heston and Carr–Madan can price options the density has no closed form for.

3.3  Conditional Expectation & the Tower Property

The conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]$ is the best forecast of $X$ given $Y$. Averaging it back recovers the unconditional mean — the law of iterated expectations (tower property):

$$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big] = \mathbb{E}[X],$$

and variance decomposes via the law of total variance

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mathbb{E}\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Y)\big] + \operatorname{Var}\big(\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]\big).$$

Poisson & Exponential Means (Q7, Q8)

Poisson: using $e^\lambda=\sum_{k\ge0}\lambda^k/k!$,

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} k\,\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!} = \lambda e^{-\lambda}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} = \lambda e^{-\lambda}e^{\lambda} = \lambda.$$

Exponential: integrating by parts, $\;\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_0^\infty x\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,dx = \dfrac{1}{\lambda}.$

Binomial MGF → Mean & Variance (Q9)

By the binomial theorem,

$$M_X(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\big(pe^t\big)^k(1-p)^{n-k} = \big(pe^t + 1-p\big)^n.$$

Then $\mathbb{E}[X]=M_X'(0)=np$ and $\operatorname{Var}(X)=M_X''(0)-M_X'(0)^2=np(1-p)$.

5  Limit Theorems

Chebyshev's Inequality

For any distribution with finite mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$,

$$P\big(|X-\mu|\ge a\big) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{a^2}.$$

A distribution-free bound on how much probability can sit in the tails.

Law of Large Numbers

For i.i.d. $X_1,\dots,X_n$ with mean $\mu$, the sample mean converges to $\mu$:

$$\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i \;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\; \mu.$$

Why averages of many draws stabilize — the basis of Monte Carlo.

Central Limit Theorem

For i.i.d. draws with mean $\mu$, variance $\sigma^2$, the standardized sum is asymptotically normal:

$$\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1).$$

Why the normal is everywhere — and why MC error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$.

Geometric Probability — Meeting on the Bridge (Q4)

Two friends arrive at independent uniform times in a 3-hour ($T=180$ min) window and each waits $w$ minutes. Plot the two arrival times as a point in a $180\times180$ square; they meet when $|X-Y|\le w$. The favourable area is the square minus two corner triangles:

$$P = \frac{T^2-(T-w)^2}{T^2}.$$

With $w=15$ this is $\dfrac{180^2-165^2}{180^2}\approx 16\%$ — the geometric route turns a probability into an area ratio.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: The limit theorems are the licence for the whole edifice of statistical finance — they say that aggregates of many small random effects behave predictably even when individual draws do not.

  • CLT & Monte Carlo: to halve simulation error you need four times the paths ($1/\sqrt{n}$).
  • Caveat: the CLT assumes finite variance — fat-tailed returns are precisely where naive normality breaks.

Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Probability rests on three axioms; equally-likely problems reduce to counting (permutations & combinations).
  • Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem are how beliefs update with evidence; independence means the joint law factorizes.
  • Random variables are described by PMF/PDF and CDF; joint laws give marginals and conditionals.
  • Expectation (linear) and variance summarize a distribution; MGFs and characteristic functions generate moments and uniquely identify the law.
  • The conditional expectation obeys the tower property and the law of total variance.
  • Know the workhorse distributions — Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson, Uniform, Normal, Exponential, Gamma, $\chi^2$ — and how they relate.
  • Chebyshev, the LLN and the CLT govern the behaviour of large samples and justify Monte Carlo.
