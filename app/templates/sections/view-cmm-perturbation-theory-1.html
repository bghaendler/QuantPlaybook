Mathematical Methods • Perturbation Theory I

Regular & Singular Perturbation Theory

Approximate solutions to problems carrying a small parameter $\epsilon$ — with motivating examples from Physics (damped oscillators), Fluid Mechanics (Navier–Stokes) and Finance (Black–Scholes near expiry & stochastic volatility). After Adnan Khan, Fitch Learning & LUMS.

1. The Basic Problem

Perturbation theory provides approximate solutions to problems that contain a small parameter $\epsilon$. It is especially effective for non-linear problems, where the small parameter is usually attached to the non-linearity.

The central requirement:

  • The limiting problem — the equation obtained by setting $\epsilon = 0$ — must be solvable.
  • We then build corrections in powers of $\epsilon$ on top of that solvable base case.

Background assumed: Calculus, Linear Algebra and Differential Equations — it is sufficient to be comfortable with the CQF primer material.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: Many real-world equations are too hard to solve exactly, but they are "almost" an easy problem plus a tiny nudge. Perturbation theory solves the easy problem first, then adds small corrections.

  • The small parameter $\epsilon$: Think of it as a dial that, when turned to zero, leaves a problem you already know how to solve.
  • Why finance cares: Short time-to-expiry options and slowly-varying volatility both produce a small $\epsilon$ you can expand around.

2. Where Small Parameters Naturally Arise

The same structure — a small $\epsilon \ll 1$ multiplying part of the equation — shows up across disciplines:

Physics — weakly damped oscillator

$$\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \epsilon\,\frac{dx}{dt} + x = 0, \qquad \epsilon \ll 1$$

Fluid Mechanics — Navier–Stokes

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\cdot\nabla u = -\nabla p + \epsilon\,\nabla^2 u, \qquad \epsilon \ll 1$$

Finance — Black–Scholes near expiry

$$\frac{1}{\epsilon^2}\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} = \tfrac{1}{2}S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + \alpha S\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \alpha V, \qquad \epsilon \ll 1$$

Note the small parameter $\epsilon$ common to all three. The art is in finding it — which is what non-dimensionalization does.

3. The Weakly Damped Oscillator & Non-Dimensionalization

From Newton's second law with a restoring spring force $-kx$ and a damping force $-\gamma v$:

$$m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \gamma\frac{dx}{dt} + kx = 0, \quad x(0)=x_0,\; \dot x(0)=v_0.$$

This "seems" to involve three parameters $m,\gamma,k$ — it is not a priori clear what is small. We use non-dimensionalization, since every physical equation must be dimensionally consistent (you can't add apples to oranges). Scaling $\tilde x = x/x_0,\; \tilde t = t/t_0$ gives

$$\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \frac{\gamma t_0}{m}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{k t_0^2}{m}\,x = 0.$$

The characteristic time $t_0$ is still free. Two natural choices:

  • (a) $t_0 = \tfrac{m}{\gamma} \Rightarrow \ddot x + \dot x + \tfrac{mk}{\gamma^2}x = 0$
  • (b) $t_0 = \sqrt{\tfrac{m}{k}} \Rightarrow \ddot x + \sqrt{\tfrac{\gamma^2}{mk}}\,\dot x + x = 0$

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: Raw equations hide which quantity is "small." Rescaling into dimensionless variables collapses several physical constants into one or two dimensionless groups — and one of those groups turns out to be your $\epsilon$.

  • Choice of clock matters: Different characteristic times $t_0$ put $\epsilon$ in different places — which is why the same system can become a regular or a singular problem.
  • Dimensional consistency: A free sanity check — every term in a correct equation carries identical units.

4. Regular vs. Singular Perturbation

Which non-dimensionalization is "better" depends on which physics dominates:

Regular Perturbation

Damping dominates ($\gamma^2 \gg mk$, choice a) or inertia dominates ($\gamma^2 \ll mk$, choice b):

$$\ddot x + \dot x + \epsilon x = 0 \qquad\text{or}\qquad \ddot x + \epsilon\dot x + x = 0$$

Setting $\epsilon=0$ leaves a well-posed second-order ODE. The order of the equation is preserved.

Singular Perturbation

A different scaling ($\tilde x = \tfrac{\gamma}{mv_0}x,\ \tilde t = \tfrac{k}{\gamma}t$) in the over-damped limit gives:

$$\epsilon\,\ddot x + \dot x + x = 0, \quad x(0)=1,\ \dot x(0)=\tfrac{1}{\epsilon}$$

Setting $\epsilon=0$ kills the highest derivative — the limiting problem is first order and cannot satisfy both initial conditions. This is the hallmark of a singular problem.

5. Navier–Stokes & the Reynolds Number

For a Newtonian fluid, Newton's second law gives

$$\rho\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\cdot\nabla u\right) = -\nabla p + \mu\nabla^2 u.$$

Scaling lengths by $L$, velocity by $U$, and pressure by $\rho U^2$ produces the dimensionless form with the Reynolds number $Re = \tfrac{UL}{\nu}$ (here $\nu = \mu/\rho$):

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\cdot\nabla u = -\nabla p + \frac{1}{Re}\nabla^2 u.$$
  • High $Re \gg 1$: $\tfrac{1}{Re}=\epsilon\ll 1$. Leading order gives the inviscid Euler equations $\;\partial_t u + u\cdot\nabla u = -\nabla p$.
  • Low $Re \ll 1$: the alternative scaling $\tilde p = pL/\mu U$ gives $\,Re(\partial_t u + u\cdot\nabla u) = -\nabla p + \nabla^2 u\,$; leading order is creeping (Stokes) flow $\,-\nabla p + \nabla^2 u = 0$.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: One equation, two regimes. The Reynolds number measures inertia vs. viscosity; whichever loses becomes the small term you expand around.

  • Fast flow (high $Re$): viscosity is the small correction — drop it and you get frictionless flow.
  • Slow / thick flow (low $Re$): inertia is the small correction — honey, glaciers, microswimmers.

6. Black–Scholes Near Expiry (a Singular Problem in Finance)

Hedging one option with $\Delta$ units of stock, $\Pi = V - \Delta S$, and applying Itô's lemma to $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dX$:

$$d\Pi = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2}\right)dt - \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \Delta\right)dS.$$

Choosing $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ hedges away all risk; no-arbitrage ($d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt$) then yields the Black–Scholes equation:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0,$$

with final conditions $V(S,T)=\max(S-K,0)$ (call) or $\max(K-S,0)$ (put). Rescaling time near expiry by $\tilde t = \sigma^2(T-t)$ and zooming in with $\tilde t = \epsilon\tau$ gives

$$\frac{1}{\epsilon}\frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} = \tfrac{1}{2}S^2\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + \alpha\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - \alpha V.$$

Because the small parameter multiplies the highest (time) derivative, this is a singular perturbation problem — the source of boundary-layer behaviour in short-dated options.

7. Order Notation & Asymptotic Expansions

To make "same size as $\epsilon$" precise (recall $\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\tfrac{\sin\epsilon}{\epsilon}=1$ by L'Hôpital), we use order notation as $\epsilon\to\epsilon_0$:

  • $f = O(g)$ ("big-oh") if $\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to\epsilon_0}\frac{f(\epsilon)}{g(\epsilon)} = K$ — $f$ and $g$ grow at the same rate.
  • $f = o(g)$ ("little-oh") if $\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to\epsilon_0}\frac{f(\epsilon)}{g(\epsilon)} = 0$ — $f$ vanishes faster than $g$.

E.g. $f(\epsilon)=3\epsilon^2+2\epsilon$ is $O(\epsilon)$ and $o(\sqrt\epsilon)$ as $\epsilon\to 0$. We call $g$ an asymptotic approximation to $f$ if $f = g + o(g)$.

Functions $\varphi_1,\varphi_2,\dots$ form an asymptotic sequence if $\varphi_n = o(\varphi_m)$ for $m $$f \sim a_1\varphi_1 + a_2\varphi_2 + \cdots + a_n\varphi_n,$$

where the $\varphi_i$ are the gauge functions (often simply $\epsilon^i$).

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: Big-O and little-o are a bookkeeping language for "how big is each term." They let us write a solution as a leading piece plus ranked corrections, and know exactly what we're throwing away.

  • Asymptotic ≠ convergent: An asymptotic series can be enormously accurate with a few terms even if it diverges when summed to infinity.
  • Gauge functions: The "rulers" $\epsilon, \epsilon^2, \sqrt\epsilon,\dots$ against which each correction is measured.

8. Regular Perturbation Expansions — Algebraic Equations

For $f(x) + \epsilon g(x) = 0$ where $f(x)=0$ is solvable, substitute the ansatz $x = x_0 + \epsilon x_1 + \epsilon^2 x_2 + \cdots$ and collect powers of $\epsilon$.

Example: $x^2 + \epsilon x - 1 = 0$. Substituting and matching orders:

$$O(1):\; x_0^2 - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow x_0 = \pm 1, \qquad O(\epsilon):\; 2x_0 x_1 + x_0 = 0 \Rightarrow x_1 = -\tfrac{1}{2}.$$

So $x \sim \pm 1 - \tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon + \cdots$, which matches the Taylor expansion of the exact roots $x = \tfrac{-\epsilon \pm \sqrt{\epsilon^2+4}}{2}$. The same machinery handles transcendental coefficients such as $x^2 + e^{\epsilon x} = 5$.

9. Regular Perturbation for an IVP & Secular Terms

Consider the damped harmonic oscillator $\ddot x + \epsilon\dot x + x = 0$ with $x(0)=1,\ \dot x(0)=0$. The ansatz $x=x_0+\epsilon x_1+\cdots$ produces the asymptotic hierarchy

$$O(1):\ \ddot x_0 + x_0 = 0, \qquad O(\epsilon):\ \ddot x_1 + x_1 = -\dot x_0, \qquad O(\epsilon^2):\ \ddot x_2 + x_2 = -\dot x_1,$$

with $x_0(0)=1$, all other ICs zero. Each level has the form $\mathcal{L}x_i = f(x_{i-1},\dots)$ where $\mathcal{L} \equiv \tfrac{d^2}{dt^2} + 1$ and the right-hand side is already known. Solving order by order:

$$x_0 = \cos t, \qquad x_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\cos t - t\sin t\big),$$ $$\boxed{\; x \sim \cos t + \tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon\big(\cos t - t\sin t\big). \;}$$

The catch — secular growth: the $t\sin t$ term grows without bound, so the expansion is only valid up to $t = O(1/\epsilon)$. The exact solution $x(t)=e^{-\epsilon t/2}\big[\cos(\omega t) + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2\omega}\sin(\omega t)\big]$, $\omega=\sqrt{1-\tfrac{\epsilon^2}{4}}$, decays slowly — capturing this for all $0\le t<\infty$ requires the two-timing / multiple-scales method (Perturbation Theory II).

10. Worked Example — Projectile with Height-Dependent Gravity

A projectile feels $F = -\tfrac{mR^2 g}{(R+x)^2}$. Non-dimensionalizing gives

$$\ddot x = -\frac{1}{(1+\epsilon x)^2}, \quad \epsilon = \frac{v_0^2}{Rg}, \quad x(0)=0,\ \dot x(0)=1.$$

With $x=x_0+\epsilon x_1+\cdots$:

$$O(1):\ \ddot x_0 = -1 \Rightarrow x_0 = -\tfrac{t^2}{2}+t,$$ $$O(\epsilon):\ \ddot x_1 = 2x_0 \Rightarrow x_1 = -\tfrac{t^4}{12}+\tfrac{t^3}{3}.$$

The time to maximum height ($\dot x=0$) expands as $t_m \sim 1 + \tfrac{2}{3}\epsilon$ — the weakening of gravity with altitude keeps the projectile aloft slightly longer.

Plain English Notes

The Big Picture: The $\epsilon=0$ problem is high-school constant-gravity projectile motion. The correction quantifies how much the inverse-square law changes the answer for a fast launch.

  • Practical payoff: a closed-form correction that agrees with the numerical (exact) solution even up to $\epsilon \approx 0.25$–$0.4$.

11. Finance Application — Stochastic Volatility & the Garman Equation

Let the stock follow $dS = rS\,dt + \sigma S\,dX$ with a fast mean-reverting volatility $d\sigma = \tfrac{\alpha(\sigma)}{\epsilon}\,dt + \tfrac{\beta(\sigma)}{\sqrt\epsilon}\,dY$, $dX\cdot dY = \rho\,dt$. A European option value $V$ satisfies Garman's equation, which in operator form reads

$$\epsilon\,\mathcal{D}_0^\sigma V + \sqrt{\epsilon}\,\rho\beta\,\mathcal{D}_1\mathcal{D}_2 V + \mathcal{D}_3 V = 0,$$

with $\mathcal{D}_0^\sigma = \partial_t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2\partial_{SS} + rS\partial_S - r$,  $\mathcal{D}_1 = S\partial_S$,  $\mathcal{D}_2 = \sigma\partial_\sigma$,  $\mathcal{D}_3 = \tfrac{1}{2}\beta^2\partial_{\sigma\sigma} + \alpha\partial_\sigma$,  $\mathcal{D}_4 = S^2\partial_{SS}$. Expanding $V \sim V_0 + \epsilon^{1/2}V_1 + \epsilon V_2 + \cdots$ yields the hierarchy $\mathcal{D}_3 V_0 = 0,\ \mathcal{D}_3 V_1 = -\rho\beta\,\mathcal{D}_1\mathcal{D}_2 V_0,\dots$ The first equation forces $V_0$ (and then $V_1$) to be independent of $\sigma$, and solvability conditions ("integrate against the invariant density $p(\sigma)$") collapse the leading order to Black–Scholes at an effective volatility $\hat\sigma$.

Carrying the expansion to $O(\epsilon)$ gives the asymptotic price

$$V(S,\sigma,t) = V_{BS}(S,\hat\sigma,t) + \epsilon^{1/2}\,\rho\gamma(T-t)\,\mathcal{D}_1\mathcal{D}_4 V_{BS} + \epsilon\,F_1(\sigma)\,\mathcal{D}_4 V_{BS} + O(\epsilon^{3/2}),$$

where $\gamma$ and $F_1(\sigma)$ are integrals of the volatility coefficients. The correction terms $\mathcal{D}_1\mathcal{D}_4 V_{BS}$ and $\mathcal{D}_4 V_{BS}$ are exactly the smile / skew adjustments to a flat Black–Scholes surface.

Summary & Key Takeaways

  • Perturbation theory finds explicit approximate solutions when a small parameter $\epsilon$ is present and the $\epsilon=0$ limit is solvable.
  • Regular problems preserve the order/structure at $\epsilon=0$; singular problems lose the highest derivative (boundary layers, near-expiry options, high/low Reynolds limits).
  • Non-dimensionalization reveals the small parameter and decides whether a problem is regular or singular.
  • Order notation ($O,\,o$) and gauge functions formalize asymptotic expansions; the ansatz $x=\sum \epsilon^i x_i$ produces a solvable hierarchy $\mathcal{L}x_i = f(x_{i-1},\dots)$.
  • Naive regular expansions can develop secular terms ($t\sin t$), valid only to $t=O(1/\epsilon)$ — the motivation for Singular Perturbation Theory / multiple scales in Perturbation Theory II.
  • In finance, the same toolkit delivers near-expiry Black–Scholes asymptotics and stochastic-volatility smile corrections via the Garman equation.
